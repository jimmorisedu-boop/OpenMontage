# Editorial Principles Reference

This is OpenMontage's operational interpretation of five editing books. It paraphrases ideas and does not reproduce the books.

## Walter Murch — *In the Blink of an Eye*

Principle: when good criteria conflict, protect emotion, story, and rhythm before eye trace, screen-plane continuity, and physical-space continuity. Treat a change in thought or attention as a candidate cut point.

Ask: What should the viewer feel now? What new thought begins? Does the cut advance the story? Where is the eye before and after it?

Apply: record the intended emotion and story advance for consequential cuts. Accept a repairable continuity imperfection when it protects a stronger dramatic result.

Failure mode: using the hierarchy as arithmetic or excusing confusing cuts. Preserve all six criteria whenever possible.

## Karen Pearlman — *Cutting Rhythms: Intuitive Film Editing* / *Cutting Rhythms: Creative Film Editing*

Principle: shape rhythm through timing, pacing, and trajectory phrasing. Rhythm is the viewer's embodied experience of movement, energy, tension, and release—not average shot length.

Ask: When should the event land? How dense are changes? Where is movement and energy going? Is tension accumulating or resolving?

Apply: annotate timing intent, pacing profile, movement trajectory, and tension/release role. Preserve a pause or gesture when it completes an emotional or physical phrase.

Failure mode: cutting faster merely to feel energetic. Increase density only when the story and movement support it.

## Karel Reisz and Gavin Millar — *The Technique of Film Editing*

Principle: choose technique from dramatic function and real material. Dialogue, action, documentary, montage, and sound-led sequences require different constructions.

Ask: What job does this scene perform? What must the viewer know, anticipate, or reconsider? Is the source observational, performed, instructional, or associative?

Apply: store a dramatic function for every sequence and explain why its construction suits the material. Judge concrete before/after versions, not a universal recipe.

Failure mode: applying talking-head cleanup rules to action, documentary, or screen instruction without adaptation.

## Edward Dmytryk — *On Film Editing*

Principle: require a positive reason to cut. Protect strong performance and meaningful reaction. If smoothness and meaning conflict, make the dramatically right cut and repair mechanics afterward.

Ask: What improves at this exact cut? Is the reaction more informative than the speaker? Am I cutting because the shot feels old, or because the scene changes?

Apply: require `positive_reason` in consequential decisions. Prefer complete, fresh performance values over cosmetic matching.

Failure mode: treating every pause, mismatch, or reaction as an error to remove.

## Michael Ondaatje — *The Conversations: Walter Murch and the Art of Editing Film*

Principle: discover the work iteratively. Compare versions and treat picture, dialogue, music, silence, and sound as one construction shaped through collaboration.

Ask: What did this local change do to the whole piece? Does sound reveal or merely repeat the image? Which version best fulfills the agreed intention?

Apply: preserve versioned edit decisions, review the whole piece after local revisions, and log changed decisions instead of silently overwriting their rationale.

Failure mode: polishing isolated cuts while losing the film's larger arc.

## Decision checklist

1. State the scene's dramatic function and intended feeling.
2. State the positive reason for the cut.
3. Verify story clarity and factual context.
4. Diagnose timing, pacing, trajectory, tension, and release.
5. Check performance, reaction, eye trace, and continuity.
6. Compare with at least one plausible alternative when confidence is low.
7. Review the complete sequence after changes.
